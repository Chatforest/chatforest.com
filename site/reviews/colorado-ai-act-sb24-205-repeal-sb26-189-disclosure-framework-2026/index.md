# America's First AI Law Is Dead: How Colorado's Landmark Regulation Went From Signing to Repeal in Two Years

> Colorado's SB 24-205 was the first U.S. law to regulate high-risk AI systems. On May 14, 2026, it was replaced by a lighter disclosure framework — killed by a xAI lawsuit, a DOJ intervention, a Trump executive order, and two years of industry pressure. Here's the complete story.


## The First Law That Wasn't

On May 17, 2024, Colorado Governor Jared Polis [signed SB 24-205 into law](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) — legislation The Colorado Sun called "the nation's first comprehensive law governing the technology" ([Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2026/05/12/colorado-ai-law-rewrite-passes/)). Law firms tracking the bill described its structure as modeled on the EU AI Act's risk-tiered approach ([Davis Wright Tremaine, "Mile-High Risk"](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2024/05/colorado-enacts-first-risk-based-ai-regulation-law)). As enacted, the law required developers and deployers of "high-risk AI" systems to conduct impact assessments, implement risk management programs, report algorithmic discrimination risks, and give consumers meaningful human review rights ([SB 24-205, official bill page and text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205)).

Polis signed it. And then, within weeks, he effectively asked the legislature to kill it.

His signing statement warned SB 24-205 "creates a complex compliance regime for all developers and deployers of AI doing business in Colorado" and that regulation "applied at the state level in a patchwork across the country can have the effect to tamper innovation and deter competition in an open market," urging lawmakers to "significantly improve" the law before it took effect ([Colorado Politics, reporting on the signing statement](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/governor/organizations-urge-colorado-governor-to-veto-ai-bill-say-it-will-hurt-small-businesses/article_0ad1209a-148c-11ef-962b-d729ef9f15f9.html)). On June 13, 2024 — not immediately, but under a month after signing — he co-signed a public letter with Senate Majority Leader Robert Rodriguez and Attorney General Phil Weiser pledging to revise the law before it took effect ([Colorado Sun, June 14, 2024](https://coloradosun.com/2024/06/14/colorado-ai-bill-revisions/)).

Two years later, on May 14, 2026, [Polis signed its replacement](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189) — a disclosure framework with no duty of care, no impact assessments, and no algorithmic discrimination liability. The Colorado Sun's headline: "[Colorado's fierce two-year fight over AI regulation ends with watered-down law, little fanfare](https://coloradosun.com/2026/05/12/colorado-ai-law-rewrite-passes/)."

## What the Original Law Required

SB 24-205 defined "high-risk AI" as any system making or substantially influencing "consequential decisions" in employment, education, healthcare, housing, financial services, insurance, legal services, or government benefits ([SB 24-205, official bill page and text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205)).

Under the original law ([bill text](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf)):

**Developers** had to:
- Exercise reasonable care to protect consumers from algorithmic discrimination
- Provide deployers with comprehensive technical documentation
- Publish public statements about their AI systems
- Notify the Attorney General and all known deployers within 90 days of discovering a credible discrimination risk

**Deployers** had to:
- Implement a risk management policy aligned to NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001
- Complete impact assessments within 90 days of deployment and annually thereafter
- Disclose to consumers when AI was making or had substantially influenced a consequential decision
- Provide meaningful human review after adverse decisions

The enforcement framework was built around algorithmic discrimination — a freestanding liability standard. Colorado's Attorney General was authorized to investigate, and potential penalties ran under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, with civil penalties of up to $20,000 per violation ([xAI v. Weiser case summary, Civil Rights Litigation Clearinghouse](https://clearinghouse.net/case/48129/)).

Civil rights advocates praised it. A joint civil-society statement — signed by the Center for Democracy & Technology, Consumer Reports, EPIC, and the AFL-CIO — called SB 24-205 "a welcome step toward much-needed transparency and accountability" and said its algorithmic-discrimination standard reflected "a central tenet of our civil rights laws" ([CDT, "Joint Civil Society Statement on Colorado Senate Bill 24-205"](https://cdt.org/insights/joint-civil-society-statement-on-colorado-senate-bill-24-205/); see also [CDT's own analysis](https://cdt.org/insights/colorados-artificial-intelligence-act-is-a-step-in-the-right-direction-it-must-be-strengthened-not-weakened/) and [EPIC's](https://epic.org/colorado-is-first-state-to-pass-broad-ai-legislation/)). The framework was specifically designed to catch AI-driven bias in the decisions with the highest stakes for consumers — loans, job applications, healthcare coverage.

Industry's response was the opposite. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce, the Consumer Technology Association, and the Chamber of Progress all urged Polis to veto it ([Colorado Politics](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/governor/organizations-urge-colorado-governor-to-veto-ai-bill-say-it-will-hurt-small-businesses/article_0ad1209a-148c-11ef-962b-d729ef9f15f9.html); [Chamber of Progress veto letter, primary text](https://progresschamber.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/CO-SB-205-Veto-Letter.pdf); [U.S. Chamber of Commerce letter to Polis](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/u-s-chamber-of-commerce-letter-to-governor-polis)). Palantir disclosed in SEC filings that comparable "state-level oversight" compliance obligations "may be difficult, onerous, and costly" ([Colorado Politics, on Palantir's SEC disclosures](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/02/18/potential-clues-palantir-shares-several-risks-of-doing-business-in-colorado/)). Elon Musk's xAI took note.

## Two Years of Dismantling

The two years between signing and repeal were a sustained campaign to kill the law through every available channel — legislative, regulatory, judicial, and federal.

**May 2025:** During the regular legislative session, SB 25-318 — an attempt to amend SB 24-205 before its scheduled effective date — was tabled in committee on a 5-2 vote days before the session ended, with no revisions reaching the governor ([Colorado Sun, "Attempt to change or delay Colorado's controversial AI law killed"](https://coloradosun.com/2025/05/05/colorado-artificial-intelligence-law-killed/)). The original effective date — February 1, 2026 — was later pushed to June 30, 2026 ([Colorado Sun, "Democrats are finalizing deal on tweaks..."](https://coloradosun.com/2025/08/24/colorado-ai-law-start-date-pushed-back/)).

**Fall 2025:** A working group convened with lawmakers, the Governor's office, and the AG's office to draft a replacement approach that fed into SB 26-189 ([Colorado Politics, "Gov. Polis convenes new working group..."](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2025/10/15/gov-polis-convenes-new-working-group-to-address-colorados-lingering-ai-law-challenges/)).

**December 11, 2025:** The Trump White House issued [Executive Order 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/), which the administration's own [fact sheet](https://www.whitehouse.gov/fact-sheets/2025/12/fact-sheet-president-donald-j-trump-ensures-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence/) frames around clearing away state AI laws. The order directs the Attorney General to establish a DOJ AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws in federal court and threatens conditioning federal BEAD broadband funding on states not maintaining "onerous" AI laws ([Sidley Austin, "Unpacking the December 11, 2025 Executive Order"](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/12/unpacking-the-december-11-2025-executive-order)).

**April 9, 2026:** [xAI filed suit](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/) in U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado against AG Philip Weiser, challenging SB 24-205 on four constitutional grounds: First Amendment compelled speech, Dormant Commerce Clause, due process vagueness, and equal protection.

**April 24, 2026:** The Department of Justice moved to intervene — described by multiple law firms tracking the case as the first time the federal government had sought to invalidate a state AI law ([Barnes & Thornburg](https://btlaw.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/doj-intervenes-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination-law)). The DOJ's [complaint in intervention](https://www.justice.gov/crt/media/1437846/dl) alleged SB 24-205 violated the Equal Protection Clause by compelling AI systems to embed race- and sex-conscious decision-making through its diversity-related carve-outs.

**April 27, 2026:** The court granted a joint motion by xAI, the DOJ, and the AG to stay enforcement ([McDermott Will & Emery](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-ai-law-in-flux-comprehensive-replacement-bill-signed-after-federal-court-blocks-predecessors-enforcement/)). Colorado's law was effectively dead before the legislature acted.

**May 9 and May 12, 2026:** The legislature passed SB 26-189 by landslide margins — the House 57-6 on May 9 ([official House floor vote](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_votes/29797)), and the Senate 34-1 on May 12 when it concurred with House amendments, completing passage ([official Senate floor vote](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_votes/29868)).

**May 14, 2026:** [Polis signed it](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189). His office's statement quoted him calling it "a big step in the right direction for Colorado, and a model for the rest of the country," created "through thoughtful collaboration with input from many stakeholders including consumers, advocates, developers and more" ([Governor's office press release](https://governorsoffice.colorado.gov/governor/news/governor-polis-signs-bills-law-making-colorado-even-better-place-do-business-breaking-down); [The Center Square](https://www.thecentersquare.com/colorado/article_38978b13-eee0-4cf6-8c89-3e9a8dea6d66.html)). The posture was the inverse of 2024.

## What Replaced It

SB 26-189's substantive ADMT obligations take effect January 1, 2027 ([official bill page](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189); [Baker Botts, "Colorado Repeals and Replaces AI Act"](https://ourtake.bakerbotts.com/post/102msga/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-ai-act)). Its key move is terminological: it abandons "high-risk AI system" and "algorithmic discrimination" in favor of "automated decision-making technology" (ADMT) — any system that processes personal data to "materially influence" a "consequential decision" ([SB 26-189, official bill text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189)).

**What developers must do under SB 26-189:**

Provide each deployer with documentation including: intended and known-harmful uses, data categories used in training, known limitations, and instructions for appropriate use and human review ([SB 26-189, official bill text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189)).

**What deployers must do:**

- Provide consumer notice when covered ADMT is used in a consequential decision
- After an adverse outcome, provide within 30 days: a plain-language explanation of the system's role, the system's name/version/developer, and an explanation of consumer rights

([SB 26-189, official bill text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189); [ArentFox Schiff summary](https://www.afslaw.com/perspectives/alerts/colorado-replaces-its-landmark-ai-act-new-framework-what-developers-and))

**Consumer rights under SB 26-189:**

1. Request personal data and correction of factually incorrect data used by the ADMT
2. Request meaningful human review following an adverse decision (the reviewer must be trained, have override authority, and cannot simply default to the system's output)
3. Receive a post-adverse-decision explanation within 30 days

([SB 26-189, official bill text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189))

**What was eliminated compared to SB 24-205:**

- Duty of reasonable care to protect against algorithmic discrimination
- Mandatory risk management programs (NIST AI RMF or ISO 42001)
- Annual impact assessments within 90 days of deployment
- Mandatory self-reporting of discrimination risks to the AG
- Freestanding algorithmic discrimination liability

(Comparison based on [SB 24-205](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205) and [SB 26-189](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189) bill text; see also [Crowell & Moring's side-by-side summary](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/colorado-hits-reset-on-ai-regulation-sb-26-189-repeals-and-reenacts-the-colorado-ai-act).)

There is a 60-day notice-and-cure period before the AG can bring an enforcement action; that right-to-cure provision itself sunsets January 1, 2030, after which no cure period is required (separately, the AG's own reporting on cure usage begins January 2028 and also sunsets in 2030) ([National Law Review, "Colorado's SB 26-189: Understanding the New ADMT Law"](https://natlawreview.com/article/colorados-sb-26-189-understanding-new-automated-decision-making-technology-law)). Rulemaking is mandatory and must be completed by January 1, 2027 ([SB 26-189, official bill text](https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb26-189)).

Robert Lindgren, communications director for the Colorado AFL-CIO, said during the bill's Senate hearings: "Gone are the risk management requirements, impact assessments, annual reviews and discrimination reporting. It introduces a cure period that lets developers delay accountability" ([Tech Times, "Colorado's AI Anti-Discrimination Law Is Dead Before It Ever Took Effect"](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/316756/20260517/colorados-ai-anti-discrimination-law-dead-before-it-ever-took-effect-replaced-notice-only.htm); title per [Colorado AFL-CIO staff page](https://coaflcio.org/who-we-are/robert-lindgren)).

## What It Signals

**The EU model will not be the U.S. template.** Colorado's original law was the clearest attempt to transplant the EU AI Act's risk-classification architecture into American law. Its rapid dismantling signals that risk management mandates — impact assessments, algorithmic discrimination frameworks, mandatory self-reporting — are not politically viable at the U.S. state level under current federal conditions.

**The Trump executive order changed the calculation.** The December 2025 order created two pressure points that few states can ignore: litigation risk (the DOJ AI Litigation Task Force is operational and willing to intervene, as it did in Colorado) and funding conditionality (BEAD program grants can be withheld) ([Executive Order 14365](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/12/eliminating-state-law-obstruction-of-national-artificial-intelligence-policy/)). Even states with gubernatorial commitment to stronger AI rules must now weigh whether their laws can survive federal court challenge.

**Disclosure is the American framework.** SB 26-189's notice-and-human-review model is broadly consistent with the direction other states are moving — Connecticut has added AI-training-data disclosure duties to its privacy law ([BCLP, "Connecticut Quietly Adds AI Disclosure Mandate"](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/connecticut-quietly-adds-ai-disclosure-mandate-to-consumer-privacy-law.html)) and Texas's AI statute relies on consumer-facing transparency with AG enforcement rather than a disparate-impact standard ([Akin, "The Growing Patchwork of State AI Laws"](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/the-growing-patchwork-of-state-ai-laws-what-it-means-for-employers)). It is not consistent with the direction the EU is moving. This is a durable split.

**State fragmentation is accelerating.** Polis warned in his 2024 signing statement about a "state-by-state patchwork." The patchwork is materializing: Colorado moving to disclosure, Connecticut layering AI-training disclosure onto its privacy law, and Florida advancing companion-chatbot age-verification bills ([MultiState, "State Children's Online Safety Laws Expand to AI Chatbots"](https://www.multistate.us/insider/2026/4/30/state-childrens-online-safety-laws-expand-beyond-social-media-in-2026)). There is no federal AI legislation on the horizon.

**Industry won this round.** The compliance architecture that SB 24-205 required — impact assessments, risk management programs, discrimination self-reporting — is gone. Disclosure obligations remain, but they are manageable. The xAI lawsuit and DOJ intervention did not need to go to verdict; the threat was enough to extract capitulation at the legislative level.

## Bottom Line

Colorado's SB 24-205 never took effect. It was signed by a governor who didn't want it, opposed by an industry that fought it for two years, targeted by a presidential executive order, sued by a company controlled by the world's wealthiest person, and then effectively nullified by a court stay — all before the legislature formally replaced it.

The replacement is real: consumers in Colorado will have disclosure rights, human review rights, and an explanation right after adverse AI-driven decisions. That is not nothing.

But the compliance architecture is gone. The duty of care is gone. The impact assessments are gone. The self-reporting is gone. The algorithmic discrimination framework that civil rights advocates called the heart of the law is gone.

What The Colorado Sun called "the nation's first comprehensive law governing the technology" ([Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2026/05/12/colorado-ai-law-rewrite-passes/)) lasted two years without ever being enforced. Its successor is a disclosure law. The question now is whether any state can sustain a stronger approach against federal litigation pressure — and whether Congress will fill the space before the patchwork becomes unworkable.

---

**Update (June 11, 2026):** The April 27 enforcement stay explicitly extends to "any legislation replacing or amending" SB 24-205 enacted in that legislative session — meaning SB 26-189 is also on ice until the court resolves xAI's preliminary injunction ([Kelley Drye, "AI Regulatory Roundup"](https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ai-regulatory-roundup-recent-developments-in-colorado-connecticut-and-california); [joint motion, primary court filing](https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/941/2026/04/US_DIS_COD_1_26cv1515_Joint_MOTION_to_Vacate_Scheduling_Conference_and_S.pdf)). The PI motion timeline was widely misreported: it is not tied to the May 14 signing date. Under the court order, xAI must file its PI motion within 28 days of the Colorado AG finalizing rulemaking implementing SB 26-189 (or its predecessor). Rulemaking itself must be complete by January 1, 2027. Enforcement cannot resume until 14 days after the court rules on the PI. In practice, SB 26-189's January 1, 2027 effective date may arrive before the PI is filed, argued, and decided — potentially pushing any real enforcement into late 2027 or beyond.
